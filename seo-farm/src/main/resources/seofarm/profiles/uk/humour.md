# Humour — United Kingdom (EN-GB)
# How to be funny for a UK B2B tech audience without undermining credibility.

## The British register
British professional readers have a high tolerance for dry wit and a very low tolerance for effort.
If a joke looks like it is trying to be funny, it has already failed.
The best humour in this context is almost invisible — a sentence that makes the reader smile without stopping to notice they were supposed to.

## What works

### 1. Deadpan understatement
State an obvious disaster in a completely flat tone. The gap between the severity of the situation and the calmness of the delivery is where the comedy lives.

> "After 14 months of development, three missed deadlines, and a budget that had roughly doubled, the product launched. It worked, mostly."

> "The rewrite was scoped at three months. It shipped two years later, with a team that had turned over entirely and documentation that described a system no longer in production."

> "The contractor accepted the offer. He did not start on Monday. Or the Monday after."

---

### 2. The dry analogy
Compare a common tech behaviour to something mundane and faintly absurd. The comparison should feel inevitable once you read it.

> "Hiring only local developers in 2026 is a bit like insisting your plumber live within walking distance. Possible. Limiting. Not obviously worth it."

> "A 600-item backlog is not a roadmap. It is a wish list written by people who did not expect to be held accountable."

> "Building a bespoke internal CRM when Salesforce exists is the engineering equivalent of knitting your own office chairs. Technically impressive. Hard to justify."

---

### 3. The knowing nod
Acknowledge something everyone in the room knows is true but that no one says in polite company.

> "There is a well-known category of software project called 'nearly done'. Items enter it regularly. Very few leave."

> "At this point, a meaningful proportion of UK digital transformation programmes consist of renaming existing processes and buying a dashboard."

> "The phrase 'we'll sort the architecture out after launch' has a lower success rate than most people are willing to admit publicly."

---

### 4. The callback
Reference a statistic or claim from earlier in the post, then undercut it slightly on the way out.

If you cited the 173,000 unfilled tech roles earlier:
> "173,000 open tech roles. Someone in a hiring manager role somewhere is currently writing the job description for number 173,001."

If you mentioned a four-month average hiring timeline:
> "Four months to hire a senior engineer. During which the product did not pause. The backlog, regrettably, did not pause either."

---

### 5. Self-aware closure
Briefly acknowledge the format, then subvert it.

> "This is the section that usually ends with 'get in touch to find out more'. Instead, here is the one concrete thing worth doing this week:"

> "Yes, this section is called The Trade-Offs. No, it is not where we quietly take back everything said above."

---

## What does NOT work for this audience

❌ **Exclamation marks for comic effect.** "And then it all broke!!" is not funny. It is exhausting.
❌ **American pop culture references.** "This is the Netflix of [X]" or movie quotes. They date badly and feel imported.
❌ **Puns.** Even clever ones. They signal low effort in a professional context.
❌ **Self-deprecating company humour.** "We're not perfect either!" undermines trust. Leave it out.
❌ **Irony that could be misread as sincere.** Write for someone skimming. If the irony requires careful reading to register, it will be taken literally.
❌ **More than two light moments per post.** One well-placed dry observation lands. Three makes you appear to be performing rather than writing.

## Placement rules
- Long post (800+ words): 1–2 moments, placed at natural section breaks — never in the opening or closing
- Short post (under 600 words): 0–1 moments only
- Never in the introduction — trust needs to be established first
- Never in the closing paragraph — that is where the thought that lingers should live
- The best placement: end of a section, just before a heading transition, when the reader is about to move on anyway
