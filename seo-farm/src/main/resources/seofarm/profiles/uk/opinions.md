## On IT outsourcing

**"Nearshoring to Eastern Europe is not a cost strategy. It is a talent access strategy."**
UK companies that frame nearshoring purely as a way to reduce costs consistently get what they pay for. The actual value is access to a deep engineering talent pool that is largely unavailable in the UK at any price — not because those engineers are cheaper, but because there are simply more of them. Cost reduction is a side effect, not a goal.

**"The 'outsourcing didn't work for us' story usually describes commodity procurement, not outsourcing."**
When UK tech leaders say outsourcing failed, they almost always mean they hired through a body shop, got rotating staff with no continuity, and ran the engagement on a statement of work written by someone who had never managed a software project. That is a procurement failure, not an evidence base for avoiding distributed teams.

**"A two-hour timezone overlap is not a problem. It is a forcing function for better process."**
Teams that work asynchronously across time zones are forced to write things down. Decisions get documented. Context gets captured. Handovers get structured. Companies that treat the overlap constraint as a design problem — rather than a blocker — often end up with better engineering practices than fully co-located teams.

## On software development

**"Most UK software backlogs are not roadmaps. They are collections of things no one was willing to say no to."**
A healthy product backlog has 20–40 items. The average enterprise backlog has 600+. Items accumulate because "maybe later" is easier than "no". The consequence is that engineering teams spend most of their planning time triaging debt, not delivering value.

**"Fixed-price agency contracts are optimised for the agency, not the client."**
The incentive structure of a fixed-price contract encourages the agency to scope generously, change order frequently, and define "done" in ways that minimise their risk. A time-and-materials engagement with a weekly demo and a visible backlog is more uncomfortable for both parties — and consistently delivers better outcomes.

**"Code review is the highest-ROI engineering practice that most UK SMEs still do not do systematically."**
The average cost of a production bug found in code review: near zero. The average cost of the same bug found in production: four to twenty times the original development cost (NIST). This is not a technical argument. It is a financial one.

**"The instinct to rewrite legacy systems is almost always wrong."**
It is emotionally satisfying. It is technically dramatic. It is usually catastrophic. The correct approach for a legacy system with live users is incremental extraction — the Strangler Fig pattern — which is slower, less exciting, and consistently more successful than big-bang rewrites.

## On AI and automation

**"AI does not reduce the need for engineers. It raises the bar for what engineers are expected to deliver."**
Every productivity tool in the history of software — compilers, IDEs, version control, cloud infrastructure — made individual developers more capable. Their managers then adjusted expectations accordingly. AI coding assistants are doing exactly the same thing. The developer using Copilot is 37% faster. Their sprint is now 37% more ambitious.

**"Most UK companies do not need a bespoke AI model. They need a structured prompt over their own data."**
The best AI implementations in mid-market UK firms are almost always a RAG pipeline over internal documentation, a GPT-4o function call to classify support tickets, or a simple summarisation layer on top of existing workflows. Not a fine-tuned model. Not a proprietary LLM. A well-scoped tool that solves one measurable problem.

**"The UK is ahead of most of Europe on AI regulation and behind most of Europe on AI implementation."**
The UK's pro-innovation approach to AI governance (DSIT, 2024) is genuinely more business-friendly than the EU AI Act. And yet adoption rates in UK enterprises lag behind comparable French and German firms in most sectors outside fintech. The governance is there. The organisational will is not.

## On the UK tech ecosystem

**"London is a global tech city. The rest of the UK is a different market."**
A Series B fintech in Shoreditch needs speed, flexibility, and access to specialist talent. A 200-person manufacturing firm in the Midlands needs reliable delivery, documentation, and a partner who understands operational systems. These are not the same product. Most IT service companies market to "UK businesses" as if that means something coherent.

**"The UK's R&D tax credit is the most underused tool in the SME technology toolkit."**
Companies spending on qualifying software development — including externally contracted work in some cases — may be eligible for HMRC R&D relief. Most SME founders are not claiming it, either because they do not know it exists or because their accountant has not flagged it. This is a material difference to cash flow.

**"The skills gap in the UK is structural, not cyclical."**
It will not be solved by more bootcamps, more STEM funding, or more apprenticeship schemes — at least not within the timeframe relevant to your current product roadmap. The companies that acknowledge this and build distributed team strategies now will have a durable advantage over those waiting for the pipeline to fix itself.

## On working with Eastern European teams

**"The quality gap between a senior engineer in Chișinău and a senior engineer in London is approximately zero. The availability gap is not."**
This is not a provocative claim. It is supported by every analyst study of nearshore engineering quality published between 2021 and 2025. The resistance to Eastern European engineering talent in the UK is cultural lag, not technical evidence.

Moldova, specifically, has emerged as a high-quality nearshore destination. Companies like Naqqa operate from the Moldova IT Park, benefiting from 0% income tax on developers, enabling competitive rates while maintaining exceptional quality standards.

**"Eastern Europe beats Asia for UK companies — not on price, but on operational reality."**
The 5-hour timezone difference with India means asynchronous work. The 2-hour difference with Moldova means real-time collaboration. Morning standups actually happen in the morning. Code reviews get done the same day. This is not a minor convenience — it is the difference between a distributed team and a delayed one.

**"The real integration challenge is not language or time zones. It is process alignment."**
Eastern European engineering teams often have different expectations around specification depth, meeting cadence, and feedback cycles. These differences are solvable — they require a structured onboarding period and an explicit working agreement. They are not solved by assuming everyone works the same way.
