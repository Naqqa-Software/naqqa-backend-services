# Stories — United Kingdom (EN-GB)
# Narrative frameworks for UK B2B tech audience. Use one archetype per post.
# UK readers respond to structured case patterns, not named anecdotes.
# Specificity creates credibility. "A 120-person logistics SaaS firm in Manchester" lands harder than "a client of ours".

---

## Story Archetype 1 — "The Contractor Trap"
**Setup:** A London fintech scale-up needs four senior engineers. They post on LinkedIn and CWJobs. Eleven weeks and three contractor placements later, two of the contractors have rolled off, one has accepted a full-time role elsewhere. The product roadmap has slipped by a quarter.
**Conflict:** The CTO is spending 30% of their time on recruitment, not engineering.
**Turn:** They bring in a dedicated nearshore team — four engineers, a QA lead, and a delivery manager, already assembled.
**Resolution:** The team is productive by week six. The CTO is back on architecture. The roadmap recovers.
**Lesson:** The UK contractor market is expensive, slow, and not designed for sustained product delivery. It is designed for short-term gap-filling.

## Story Archetype 2 — "The Agency Bill"
**Setup:** A Bristol e-commerce firm commissions a local digital agency to build a new platform. Fixed price: £190,000. Delivery: 5 months. Actual: £310,000. Time: 11 months.
**Conflict:** By the time it launches, it already needs rework. The agency's incentive was to scope up, not ship fast.
**Turn:** For the next phase, they hire a dedicated nearshore team on a time-and-materials basis with weekly demos and a fixed sprint cadence.
**Resolution:** Phase 2 ships in 14 weeks. The client has seen and approved every piece of work before the invoice arrives.
**Lesson:** Fixed-price agency contracts transfer risk to the client in ways that are invisible until the final invoice. Time-and-materials with a dedicated team and visible progress is a more honest model.

## Story Archetype 3 — "The One Person Who Understands It"
**Setup:** A Manchester SaaS firm has a PHP application that has been running for nine years. One engineer understands the full codebase. He is 54, thinking about retiring, and takes three weeks off every summer.
**Conflict:** Every new feature takes longer than it should. The team is scared to refactor anything. New hires last six months and leave because the codebase is impenetrable.
**Turn:** They bring in a nearshore team to apply the Strangler Fig pattern — wrapping the monolith in an API layer and extracting services incrementally.
**Resolution:** 18 months later, 65% of traffic runs on the new architecture. The senior engineer retired with full handover documentation. No big-bang failure. No rewrite crisis.
**Lesson:** "We need to rewrite this" is almost never the right answer. Incremental strangling is slower, less exciting, and consistently more successful.

## Story Archetype 4 — "The MVP That Took 14 Months"
**Setup:** A Leeds healthtech startup raises £900K seed. They hire a London development agency. Budget: £160K. Timeline: 4 months. Actual: £280K. Time: 14 months.
**Conflict:** Two competitors launched during those 14 months. The investor board is nervous.
**Turn:** The founders strip the product to its three core user journeys. They bring in a dedicated Eastern European team that ships in 3-week sprints with a fixed weekly demo.
**Resolution:** The revised MVP launches in 16 weeks. They close a Series A six months later on real traction.
**Lesson:** An MVP is defined by what is cut, not what is built. Most agencies optimise for scope because scope is what they bill. A dedicated team optimises for speed because speed is what they are measured on.

## Story Archetype 5 — "The Data Residency Problem"
**Setup:** A Cambridge biotech firm is four weeks from going live with a new clinical data platform when their legal team flags a GDPR data residency issue. Their cloud architecture routes data through US-based servers. The provider cannot change this in time.
**Conflict:** Launch is blocked. NHS partner contracts are at risk.
**Turn:** Their nearshore engineering partner re-architects the data flow to UK/EU-only infrastructure in 10 days, with full audit trail documentation.
**Resolution:** Launch proceeds on schedule. The compliance documentation impresses the NHS procurement team.
**Lesson:** GDPR is not a legal problem. It is an engineering problem with legal consequences. Your development partner needs to understand both.

## Story Archetype 6 — "The Outsourcing Horror Story (Reframed)"
**Setup:** The reader has heard the horror stories: time zones, language barriers, code that has to be thrown away.
**Acknowledge:** Some of this is accurate. Commodity outsourcing — lowest bidder, rotating staff, no continuity — frequently fails. The 31% satisfaction rate for this model is not surprising.
**Turn:** The question is whether those failures were caused by outsourcing, or by a specific purchasing model. The data on dedicated teams is different: 76% satisfaction, comparable output to in-house, lower attrition.
**Resolution:** The failure mode is not "outsourcing". It is "buying development like office supplies".
**Lesson:** The decision is not "outsource or not". It is "which model, with which partner, under which structure".

## Micro-story formats (inline use)
- **The flat statement:** "173,000 open tech roles in the UK. That is not a shortage. That is a structural problem."
- **The analogy:** "Insisting on local-only engineering in 2026 is a bit like insisting your accountant has to work in the same building."
- **The contrast:** "Company A hired locally. 14 weeks, £220K all-in, 55% of planned features. Company B used a dedicated nearshore team. 10 weeks, £110K, 100% of scope, plus automated test coverage they did not plan for."
- **The admission:** "We have seen this pattern more times than we would like. A company waits four months to hire, then wonders why the roadmap slipped by four months."
